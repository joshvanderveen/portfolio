Searching for a Shopify developer turns up freelancer marketplaces, overseas agencies, Shopify's own Experts directory and a lot of studios that all say the same thing. The hard part isn't finding someone. It's knowing whether they'll build a store that sells, and whether you'll still be able to run it when they've moved on.

This guide covers when you actually need a developer, the eight questions that sort the good ones from the rest, and the warning signs to walk away from.

## Do you need a developer at all?

Shopify is designed so you can set up a basic store yourself. If you have a handful of products, a stock theme looks fine and you're happy adding products and pages, start there.

You need a developer when:

- A stock theme can't do what your brand or products need, and you want a custom theme or real design changes.
- You're moving from WooCommerce, Squarespace, Wix or an old platform and don't want to lose products, customers or search rankings.
- You need your store to talk to something else, such as accounting, shipping, a warehouse, a booking system or a loyalty program.
- Your current store is slow, hard to update, or was built by someone who isn't around any more.

## The eight questions

### 1. Will you build a custom theme or customise a stock one, and why?

Both are valid. A customised stock theme costs less and launches faster. A custom theme fits your brand and products exactly and is easier to keep tidy over time. A good developer will tell you which suits your budget and goals, not default to the expensive option.

### 2. Can I see stores you've built and speak to the owners?

Look at the stores on a phone, because that's where most of your customers will be. Then ask the owners two things: did the project land on time and budget, and what happened the first time they needed a change after launch.

### 3. How do you quote, and what's included?

Ask for a fixed price with a written scope. Hourly estimates on a store project tend to grow. The scope should say which pages, which features, whether products are loaded for you, and what happens if you ask for something extra halfway through.

### 4. Who owns the store and the code?

The Shopify account should be in your name, on your email, with your card. The developer gets collaborator access to it. If a developer wants to set the store up under their own account "to make it easier", that's a no. Ask the same about the theme code: it should be yours to keep, whoever maintains it later.

### 5. How will you handle the migration?

If you're moving platforms, ask how products, customers and order history come across, what gets lost, and how old page addresses will redirect to the new ones. Redirects are what keep the search rankings you've built. A developer who hasn't thought about them will cost you traffic.

### 6. What happens after launch?

Shopify changes, apps update, and you'll want things tweaked. Ask whether support is included, what a monthly plan covers, and how quickly small changes get done. "Email us and it's done" is a different answer from "send a brief and we'll quote it".

### 7. How fast will it be on a phone?

Slow stores lose sales, especially on mobile. Ask how they handle images, how many apps they'll install and whether they test on real phones. If they can't answer in plain language, keep looking.

### 8. Which apps will you add, and what do they cost each month?

Apps are the hidden cost of Shopify. Each one adds a monthly fee and often slows the store down. A good developer uses as few as possible, tells you the running costs up front, and builds the feature instead when that's cheaper over a year.

## Red flags

- The store or domain is registered under the developer's account.
- No preview store or staging link while the work is happening.
- "Guaranteed first page on Google."
- Designs shown only on desktop.
- You can't get a straight answer about who owns what.
- The quote is a single number with no written scope.

## Local, overseas or a marketplace?

Marketplaces like Upwork and Airtasker are cheapest for a small, well-defined task, such as a theme fix. Overseas agencies can be cheaper for a full build but you'll manage time zones, briefs and quality yourself. A local developer costs more per hour but you can get them on the phone, they understand Australian shipping, GST and payment options, and they're still there in a year.

For a store that matters to your business, pay for the one you can reach.
